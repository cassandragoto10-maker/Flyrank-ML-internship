## CHAPTER 1: Entrepreneurship — Evolution and Revolution

**Key concepts**
- Entrepreneurs as agents of change; entrepreneurship as a wellspring of economic growth, social renewal, personal development.
- Distinction between **business entrepreneurs** (profit-driven, growth/market share) and **social entrepreneurs** (mission-driven, solve problems markets/public sector don't address).
- Distinction between **entrepreneurs** and **small-business owners** (small-business owners exploit existing equilibrium opportunities; entrepreneurs create new markets).
- **Macro view** vs **micro view** of entrepreneurship (external locus of control vs internal locus of control).
- Seven schools of entrepreneurial thought.
- **Gazelles** vs "mice" firms.
- National culture and entrepreneurship (Hofstede, Lee & Peterson's six cultural dimensions).
- Collective entrepreneurship vs individualistic "ideal type."

**Definitions/terminology**
- *Entrepreneur* (French *entreprendre* = "to undertake"): innovator/developer who recognises and seizes opportunities, converts them into workable ideas, adds value through time/effort/money/skills, assumes risk, and realises rewards.
- *Entrepreneurship*: dynamic process of vision, change, and creation requiring energy/passion to create and implement new ideas; four dimensions — individual, organisational, social/cultural, process.
- *Enterprising*: "marked by imagination, initiative, and readiness to undertake new projects."
- *Entrepreneurial*: "willing to take risks in order to create value."
- *School of thought*: a body of belief/literature/theory accepted as authoritative by a group of scholars.
- *Corridor principle*: new pathways/opportunities arise that lead entrepreneurs in different directions; preparation + opportunity = "luck."
- *Gazelle* (David Birch): business with ≥20% sales growth/year for 5 years, starting from a base of at least $100,000.
- *Displacement* (political, cultural, economic): forces that push individuals out of other activities into entrepreneurship.
- *Collective entrepreneurship*: entrepreneurial function filled cooperatively rather than by one person (Schumpeter).

**Schools of entrepreneurial thought (Figure 1.2)**
- Macro view (external locus of control): Social & cultural school; Financial/capital school; Displacement school; Ecological school.
- Micro view (internal locus of control): Entrepreneurial trait school ("people school"); Venture opportunity school; Strategic formulation school.
- Strategic formulation "unique elements": unique markets (mountain vs mountain gap), unique people (great chef), unique products (better widget), unique resources (water well).

**Formulas/frameworks**
- No mathematical formula in Ch.1 itself, but conceptual definitions (see Ch.2 for Entrepreneurship = f(entrepreneur)).

**Processes**
- Evolution of entrepreneurship through history: Assyria/Phoenicia → Rome (freedmen route) → medieval China (state ownership blocked enterprise) → Islam (permitted commerce) → medieval Europe (military wealth, then merchant capitalism) → Industrial Revolution.
- Early economic definitions progression: Cantillon (1725, risk-bearing) → Say (1803) → Schumpeter (1934) → Ronstadt's summary definition → authors' integrated definition.

**Examples**
- Case 1.1: Paul Cave / BridgeClimb Sydney Harbour Bridge — monopolising an icon he doesn't own; word-of-mouth marketing; "enable customers to make heroes of themselves."
- Climate entrepreneurs: Faroukh Mia (Bangladesh fisherman using solar/microcredit via Grameen Shakti).
- New Zealand "lifestyle entrepreneurs" (Burke Publishing) vs American growth-driven entrepreneurs — "tall poppy syndrome."

**Important relationships/numbers**
- 2009 Global Entrepreneurship Monitor (GEM): ~1 in 11 adults worldwide starting/managing a business.
- Start-up rates 2008: Philippines 39.2%, Thailand 28.4%, China 24.6%, down to Japan 2.2% (Table 1.1).
- High-growth entrepreneurs (% of all entrepreneurs) 2008: Hong Kong 21.4%, Singapore 20.4% highest; India 3.0% lowest (Table 1.2).
- Only ~8% of start-ups expect to create 20+ jobs; only ~3% expect 50+ jobs (most are "lifestylers").
- Self-employment rates by profession: Farmers 80%, Artists 62%, Real estate brokers 61% (Table 1.3).
- Entrepreneurship education: grew from ~20 US schools two decades prior to 2000+ US schools; ~45 dedicated refereed journals.

**Likely assessment points**

- List/explain the seven schools of thought (macro vs micro).
- Explain the gazelle concept and its five myths.
- Explain Lee & Peterson's six cultural dimensions.
- Trends in entrepreneurship research and education (bullet list in chapter).

## CHAPTER 2: The Entrepreneurial Mind-Set

**Key concepts**
- Global demographics of entrepreneurs (age distribution, GEM 2008 data).
- Common characteristics of successful entrepreneurs (commitment, achievement drive, opportunity orientation, initiative, persistence, feedback-seeking, internal locus of control, tolerance for ambiguity, calculated risk-taking, integrity, tolerance for failure, energy, creativity, vision, independence, team-building).
- "Dark side" of entrepreneurship — failed/criminal entrepreneurs (opportunists, failed inventors, empire-builders, comeback entrepreneurs, controversy-attractors, dishonest entrepreneurs).
- Four types of entrepreneurial risk.
- Entrepreneurial stress and Type A behaviour.
- Entrepreneurial ego's destructive potential.
- Model of entrepreneurial motivation.
- Mind-set differences: business vs social vs sustainability entrepreneurs.

**Definitions/terminology**
- *Entrepreneur* (Bolton & Thompson): "a person who habitually creates and innovates to build something of recognised value around perceived opportunities."
- *Entrepreneurship = f(entrepreneur)* — simple theoretical formula stating entrepreneurship is a function of the entrepreneur.
- *Opportunity orientation*: constant awareness of opportunities in everyday life; goal-oriented pursuit of them.
- *Internal locus of control*: belief that outcomes are governed by one's own actions, not fate/luck.
- *Type A behaviour*: personality marked by time urgency, multiple simultaneous projects, neglect of non-work life, excessive responsibility-taking, fast/explosive speech; linked in the literature to stress and coronary heart disease.
- *Financial risk / career risk / family and social risk / psychic risk* — the four areas of entrepreneurial risk.
- *Social entrepreneur*: "people with the mission to create and sustain social value."
- *Gap filling* (Kalish): business entrepreneurs plug "structural holes" between similar (homophilous) groups for personal gain; social entrepreneurs span holes between competing (heterophilous) groups for the benefit of society.

**Formulas/frameworks**
- Entrepreneurship = f(entrepreneur).
- Figure 2.2 Model of Entrepreneurial Motivation: Personal Characteristics (PC), Personal Environment (PE), Personal Goals (PG), Business Environment (BE), and business idea → decision to behave entrepreneurially → entrepreneurial strategy → entrepreneurial management → firm outcomes → expectation/outcome comparison → intrinsic/extrinsic rewards (feedback loop).
- Four causes of entrepreneurial stress (Boyd & Gumpert): loneliness, immersion in business, people problems, need to achieve.

**Processes**
- The "entrepreneurial journey": emergence of opportunity, emergence of venture, and emergence of the entrepreneur occur simultaneously/interactively (not linear, not predetermined).
- Sources of research on entrepreneurs: technical/professional journals, textbooks, trade books, biographies, compendia, news periodicals, venture periodicals, newsletters, conference proceedings, Internet, direct observation (interviews/case studies), speeches/presentations.

**Examples**
- Table 2.3: Hornaday's 42 characteristics attributed to entrepreneurs.
- "Entrepreneurial Fear 101" — Wilson Harrell (Kraft food brokerage).
- BBC "Mind of a Millionaire" — three entrepreneur types: social, theme, serial — motivated by revenge, status, or power.

**Important relationships/numbers**
- 2008 GEM: 43 countries, 2 billion population surveyed, 133 million entrepreneurs (~10.5% of population) (Table 2.1).
- Highest entrepreneurship age bracket: 25–34, then 35–44.
- Region: up to 45% of entrepreneurs are 18–34 (China/Thailand highest; NZ/Australia ~one-third) (Table 2.2).
- Harding: 3.2% of world adult population are social entrepreneurs (~139.5 million people 18–64) [note: also appears/repeated in Ch.4].

**Likely assessment points**
- List and explain the ~16 common entrepreneurial characteristics.
- Explain the four types of risk.
- Explain the four causes of stress and four "ego" traits with destructive potential.
- Diagram/explain the model of entrepreneurial motivation (PC/PE/PG/BE).
- Compare mind-set of business vs social vs sustainability entrepreneurs (gap-filling theory).

## CHAPTER 3: The Environment, the Economy and Entrepreneurship

**Key concepts**
- Entrepreneurship during economic crisis/recession ("creative destruction," Schumpeter).
- Climate change effects on entrepreneurs in the Asia–Pacific.
- Five basic global problems affecting entrepreneurial opportunity: population, water, biodiversity, food/energy (energy folded into "Energy and climate" section).
- Virtual water trade.
- Climate change economics: externalities, tragedy of the commons, market failure, emissions trading (cap and trade), peak resource theory, mitigation vs adaptation, climate risk analysis, social discount rate, equity vs efficiency, free trade and the environment.
- The Sub-prime mortgage crisis (2008–09) as case of market/ethical failure.
- Frameworks in "entrepreneurial ecology": natural capitalism, cradle-to-cradle design, economic gardening, the Natural Step, industrial metabolism, natural advantage of nations, lean manufacturing/seven wastes (Muda), ecology of commerce.

**Definitions/terminology**
- *Positive externality*: uncompensated benefit to third parties (e.g., tourism benefiting from well-kept farms).
- *Negative externality*: uncompensated cost imposed on third parties (e.g., greenhouse gas emissions) — ranked the #1 externality of economic activity.
- *Tragedy of the commons* (Hardin): shared, unowned resources get over-exploited because individual gain is private while damage is shared.
- *Emissions trading / cap and trade*: government sets a cap on emissions, issues tradeable permits; firms that can cut emissions cheaply sell permits to those that cannot.
- *Peak resource theory* (Hubbert): predicts depletion of finite resources; a "peak curve" applies to any resource harvested faster than it is replaced.
- *Mitigation* (inside-out strategy: reduce own emissions/impact) vs *Adaptation* (outside-in strategy: adjust to a changing climate/market).
- *Social discount rate*: economic weighting used to value future costs/benefits (e.g., future lives lost to rising seas) against present costs.
- *Virtual water trade*: water "embedded" in the production of goods/services, traded indirectly via traded products.
- *Natural capitalism* (Hawken, A. Lovins, H. Lovins): treats the economy as embedded within the ecosystem; 4 strategies — resource conservation, closed-loop reuse, quality-over-quantity value shift, investment in natural capital.
- *Cradle-to-cradle design* (McDonough & Braungart): 3 principles — use current solar income; waste equals food; celebrate diversity — eliminating "waste" via continuous material cycling.
- *Economic gardening*: growing local entrepreneurial capacity (vs. "hunting" for external investment) via public–private collaboration and strategic industrial clusters.
- *The Natural Step*: Swedish (1989) systems-thinking framework; four non-overlapping sustainability principles used by firms/municipalities.
- *Industrial metabolism* (Ayres): "the whole integrated collection of physical processes that convert raw materials and energy, plus labour, into finished products and wastes" — analogised to a living organism.
- *Natural advantage of nations*: Australian framework building on Porter's "Competitive Advantage of Nations"; shows economic growth and environmental improvement can be decoupled from harm.
- *Lean manufacturing / Muda (7 wastes)*: rework, overproduction, conveyance, waiting, inventory, motion, overprocessing (Toyota Production System).
- *Ecology of commerce* (Paul Hawken): the market itself, through innovation, can reverse environmental degradation by making waste/pollution costly.
- *Necessity entrepreneurs*: people who start businesses from lack of alternative (common in high-population developing countries).

**Formulas/frameworks**
- Stern Review: cost of acting on climate now ≈ 1% of GDP/year; cost of inaction ≈ 5% GDP/year (up to 20% if all risk factors hit at once).
- Hubbert peak curve (Figure 3.5) — production rises, peaks, then declines as reserves deplete.
- "Peak" timeline estimates (Table 3.4): Peak oil ~2010, Peak water ~2025, Peak coal ~2025, Peak gas ~2030, Peak uranium ~2035.
- 4 central strategies of natural capitalism; 3 principles of cradle-to-cradle; 4 principles of the Natural Step; 7 wastes of lean manufacturing (Muda).

**Processes**
- Cap-and-trade mechanism: government sets cap → issues permits → penalties for violations → market trading of permits → investment incentive in low-emission technology.
- Creative process from crisis: recession/crisis → collapse of old assumptions → entrepreneurial innovation → recovery (historical examples: GE fluorescent bulb in Depression, McDonald's in Eisenhower recession, Microsoft in 1973 oil crisis/Vietnam War, Apple/WWW in Gulf War, iPod after dotcom crash/9-11).

**Examples**
- Indonesia's palm oil/rainforest burning — entrepreneurial poverty relief vs. environmental disaster; Indonesia ranked 14th in global carbon emissions.
- Fiji Water case ("Entrepreneurial Edge" box) — exporting bottled water while 1/3 of Fijians lack clean drinking water.
- Water statistics: coffee cup = 140 L water; 1 L milk = 1000 L water; 1 kg beef = 15,500 L water; 1 kg chicken = 3,000 L water.
- 18 of the 30 Dow Jones companies launched during recessions/bear markets.

**Important relationships/numbers**
- World population ~6.76 billion (2009); Asia ~3.8 billion (>60% of world population); China 20%, India 16% of world population.
- By 2025, >35% of world population will live in water-stressed regions.
- Freshwater = only ~2.5% of total water volume; of that, 68.9% is ice/snow, 30.8% groundwater, 0.3% lakes/rivers.
- WHO: every $1 invested in water/sanitation returns $3–$34.
- Emissions trading market worth $47 billion in 2007 (Asia ~80% of it).
- China's CO2 emissions grew 66.2% since 2000.

**Likely assessment points**
- Explain positive vs negative externality and the tragedy of the commons with an example.
- Explain how cap-and-trade works and its main allocation controversy.
- Define peak resource theory and list the "peak" years.
- Distinguish mitigation vs adaptation strategy.
- Explain and compare the 8 frameworks of "entrepreneurial ecology" (natural capitalism, cradle-to-cradle, economic gardening, Natural Step, industrial metabolism, natural advantage of nations, lean/Muda, ecology of commerce).
- Explain why entrepreneurship tends to rise during recessions (Schumpeterian view).

**Key concepts**

**Definitions/terminology**
- *Innovation* (Kevin Hindle): "the combination of an inventive process and an entrepreneurial process to create new economic value for defined stakeholders."
- *Creativity*: "the generation of ideas that results in the improved efficiency or effectiveness of a system."
- *Disruptive technology* (Christensen): a lower-performance/cheaper product/process that gains a foothold in the low end of a market and moves upmarket, eventually displacing incumbents.
- *Adaptor* vs *Innovator* (Kirton, Table 5.4): Adaptor = disciplined, refines current practice, means-oriented; Innovator = unconventional angles, questions assumptions, ends-oriented, low tolerance for routine.
- *Lateral thinking* (de Bono): generative thinking that breaks "concept prisons" of old ideas; complements (not replaces) *vertical thinking*, which is selective/sequential/logical.
- Four-phase creative process (Figure 5.1): (1) Knowledge accumulation; (2) Incubation; (3) Idea experience ("eureka factor"); (4) Evaluation and implementation.
- *Invention*: totally new product/service/process (revolutionary).
- *Extension*: expansion/new application of an existing product/service/process.
- *Duplication*: creative replication of an existing concept, improved to beat competition.
- *Synthesis*: combination of existing concepts/factors into a new formulation.
- *Social innovation* (Stanford definition): "a novel solution to a social problem that is more effective, more efficient, more sustainable, or more just than existing solutions and for which the value created accrues primarily to society as a whole rather than to private individuals."

# DRAFT STUDY NOTES — RUN 1

## Chapter 1 — Entrepreneurship: Evolution and Revolution
Entrepreneurship is defined as a dynamic process of vision, change, and creation, requiring energy and passion to implement new ideas. An entrepreneur is an innovator who recognises and seizes opportunities, converts them into workable ideas, adds value, assumes risk, and captures reward. The chapter distinguishes **business entrepreneurs** (profit-driven) from **social entrepreneurs** (mission-driven) and from **small-business owners**, who tend to maintain rather than grow their ventures.

Entrepreneurial thought is organised into a **macro view** (external forces: social/cultural, financial/capital, displacement, and ecological schools) and a **micro view** (internal factors: trait/people school, venture opportunity school, strategic formulation school). Historically, entrepreneurship's status has shifted across civilisations — tolerated and prosperous in Phoenicia and the Islamic world, suppressed under Roman aristocratic norms and medieval Chinese state ownership, and eventually enabled by European merchant capitalism and the Industrial Revolution.

The chapter introduces **gazelles** — high-growth firms (20%+ annual growth for five years from a $100,000 base) — and debunks five myths about them (that they're everyone's goal, VC-backed, never started small, high-tech, and global). National GEM data shows widely varying start-up rates across the Asia–Pacific (Philippines and Thailand highest, Japan lowest), and cultural dimensions (Hofstede; Lee & Peterson) help explain cross-country differences in entrepreneurial orientation.

## Chapter 2 — The Entrepreneurial Mind-Set
This chapter profiles entrepreneurs psychologically. Common traits include commitment/perseverance, achievement drive, opportunity orientation, initiative, persistent problem-solving, feedback-seeking, internal locus of control, tolerance for ambiguity, calculated (not reckless) risk-taking, integrity, tolerance for failure, high energy, creativity, vision, independence, self-confidence, and team-building ability — summarised in Hornaday's list of 42 characteristics.

Entrepreneurship also has a **"dark side"**: an overbearing need for control, distrust, an overriding desire for success, and unrealistic optimism can each become destructive when taken to an extreme. Entrepreneurs face four categories of risk — **financial, career, family/social, and psychic** — and four sources of stress: loneliness, immersion in business, people problems, and the need to achieve, sometimes compounded by "Type A" personality traits.

The simple theoretical formula **Entrepreneurship = f(entrepreneur)** frames much of the chapter's psychological approach. A fuller **model of entrepreneurial motivation** (Figure 2.2) links personal characteristics, personal environment, personal goals, and the business environment to a decision to act entrepreneurially, followed by strategy, management, outcomes, and comparison against expectations (feedback loop). The chapter closes by distinguishing the mind-set of social and sustainability entrepreneurs from business entrepreneurs, notably through Kalish's "gap-filling" theory (business entrepreneurs bridge similar/homophilous groups for personal gain; social entrepreneurs bridge competing/heterophilous groups for social benefit).

## Chapter 3 — The Environment, the Economy and Entrepreneurship
Historically, recessions and crises have catalysed entrepreneurial innovation (Schumpeter's "creative destruction") — GE's fluorescent bulb, McDonald's, Microsoft, Apple, and the iPod all launched during downturns. The chapter frames climate change as both a crisis caused substantially by entrepreneurial activity and an arena of enormous entrepreneurial opportunity, especially across five interlocking global problems: **population, water, biodiversity**, and **energy** (via climate change economics).

Key economic concepts include **positive and negative externalities**, the **tragedy of the commons** (shared, unowned resources are over-exploited because gains are private and costs are shared), **emissions trading/cap-and-trade** as a market mechanism to price pollution, and **peak resource theory** (Hubbert), which forecasts depletion timelines for oil, water, coal, gas, and uranium. Firms must choose between **mitigation** (reducing their own impact) and **adaptation** (adjusting to a changed climate/market), informed by climate risk analysis and the ethically loaded **social discount rate**.

The chapter closes with eight frameworks for "entrepreneurial ecology": **natural capitalism**, **cradle-to-cradle design**, **economic gardening**, **the Natural Step**, **industrial metabolism**, **natural advantage of nations**, **lean manufacturing/the seven wastes (Muda)**, and **ecology of commerce** — all attempts to redesign economic activity so it works with, rather than against, natural systems.

## Chapter 4 — Ethical, Environmental and Social Entrepreneurship
Ethics is defined as the code of acceptable conduct in society; morals are the values of an individual defining a good life. A 2×2 framework (ethical/legal) helps classify entrepreneurial decisions, and a typology of "morally questionable acts" (non-role, role failure, role distortion, role assertion) explains how entrepreneurs rationalise unethical behaviour "for the firm."

**Ecopreneurs** combine entrepreneurial drive with environmental stewardship; Walley and Taylor's typology places them along two axes (economic vs sustainability orientation; soft vs hard structural influences) into four types: Innovative Opportunist, Visionary Champion, Ethical Maverick, and Ad Hoc Enviropreneur.

**Social entrepreneurship** is mission-driven innovation to solve problems the market and public sector fail to address. Social ventures progress through four growth stages (Apprenticeship, Launch, Take-off, Maturity) and take one of three forms (leveraged non-profit, hybrid non-profit, social business venture), sitting somewhere on a continuum between pure social value and pure economic value.

The chapter also surveys **disadvantaged entrepreneurs** — barriers span social/individual, geographic, community, cultural, economic, political/structural, and organisational categories — and argues, via social marginality theory, that marginalisation can itself be a driver of entrepreneurial behaviour, illustrated with global examples (Nepal, Siberia, Kerala, the Maldives) and a survey of Indigenous entrepreneurship.

## Chapter 5 — Innovation: The Creative Pursuit of Ideas
Innovation is "the combination of an inventive process and an entrepreneurial process to create new economic value" (Hindle) — most entrepreneurs commercialise others' inventions rather than inventing themselves. Opportunity identification draws on eight sources: trends, unexpected occurrences, incongruities, process needs, industry/market changes, demographics, perceptual changes, and knowledge-based concepts.

Creativity follows a four-phase process — **knowledge accumulation, incubation, the idea experience ("eureka factor"), and evaluation/implementation** — and can be supported by techniques like **lateral thinking** (de Bono), which complements traditional **vertical (logical/sequential) thinking**. Kirton's adaptor/innovator distinction describes two different creative problem-solving styles.

Four types of innovation are defined by originality: **invention, extension, duplication, synthesis**, illustrated with numerous Asia–Pacific examples (Victa lawn-mower, Cochlear bionic ear, black box flight recorder, penicillin, bungy jumping). Christensen's **disruptive technology** concept explains how lower-cost/lower-performance innovations displace incumbents from the bottom of a market upward. ## Chapter 1: Entrepreneurship — Evolution and Revolution

**1. Topic:** The historical evolution of entrepreneurship, definitions of "entrepreneur" and "entrepreneurship," schools of entrepreneurial thought, and entrepreneurship's role in the modern (Asia–Pacific) economy.

**2. Key definitions**
- **Entrepreneur**: innovator/developer who recognises and seizes opportunities, converts them into workable/marketable ideas, adds value through time/effort/money/skills, assumes competitive-market risk, and realises the rewards.
- **Entrepreneurship**: a dynamic process of vision, change, and creation requiring energy and passion to implement new ideas; spans individual, organisational, social/cultural, and process dimensions.
- **Business entrepreneur** (profit-driven) vs **Social entrepreneur** (mission-driven) vs **Small-business owner** (exploits existing markets rather than creating new ones).
- **Gazelle**: firm with ≥20% annual sales growth for 5 years from a base of ≥$100,000.
- **Corridor principle**: new opportunities emerge as a byproduct of pursuing an existing venture.

**3. Main concepts**
- Macro view of entrepreneurship (external locus of control): Social & cultural, Financial/capital, Displacement, Ecological schools of thought.
- Micro view (internal locus of control): Entrepreneurial trait ("people") school, Venture opportunity school, Strategic formulation school.
- Displacement types: political, cultural, economic.
- Strategic formulation "unique elements": unique markets, unique people, unique products, unique resources.
- National culture and entrepreneurship: Hofstede's dimensions; Lee & Peterson's six cultural dimensions (uncertainty avoidance, power distance, materialism, individualism, achievement, behaviour regulation).
- Collective entrepreneurship (function filled cooperatively, not just by one "rugged individualist").
- Five gazelle myths (not everyone's goal; not always VC-funded; not always "born" gazelles; not only high-tech; not necessarily global).
- Trends in entrepreneurship research: social entrepreneurship, entrepreneurial cognition, women/minority entrepreneurs, family business research, venture financing, intrapreneurship, cross-disciplinary growth, "know-who"/networks, career risk & stress research.
- Trends in entrepreneurship education: rapid growth in courses/programs; tension between "compliance" business-school culture and creativity/innovation focus.

**4. Important formulas**
- None numeric; conceptual "formula": entrepreneur = f(opportunity recognition + value conversion + resource commitment + risk assumption + reward capture).

**5. Processes/steps**
- Historical evolution: ancient trading civilisations (Phoenicia) → Roman restriction (nobility barred from commerce) → medieval Chinese state monopoly on property → Islamic permission of trade → medieval European shift from military to merchant wealth → Industrial Revolution.
- Development of definitions: Cantillon (risk-bearing, 1725) → Say (1803) → Schumpeter (1934) → Ronstadt's synthesis → the authors' integrated definition (vision, change, creation).

**6. Examples**
- Case 1.1: Paul Cave / BridgeClimb — monopolised access to the Sydney Harbour Bridge, built a $50m business through word-of-mouth marketing.
- Faroukh Mia (Bangladesh) — solar-powered "climate entrepreneur," microcredit via Grameen Shakti.
- Burke Publishing (NZ) — "lifestyle entrepreneurs" living/working from a sailboat, illustrating cultural attitudes to growth ("tall poppy syndrome").

**7. Important points to remember**
- Entrepreneurship ≠ small business ownership; growth/opportunity-creation is the defining difference.
- Most start-ups are "lifestyle" ventures, not gazelles (only ~8% expect 20+ jobs; ~3% expect 50+).
- The Asia–Pacific shows enormous variation in start-up rates (Philippines 39.2% vs Japan 2.2%, 2008 GEM data).
- Entrepreneurship theory is genuinely interdisciplinary — no single school of thought fully explains it.

**2. Key definitions**

- **Opportunity orientation**: constant awareness of and goal-directed pursuit of opportunities.
- **Internal locus of control**: belief that outcomes are governed by one's own actions rather than external forces.
- **Type A behaviour**: time-urgent, multi-project, work-obsessed personality pattern linked to stress/heart disease.
- **Financial, career, family/social, and psychic risk** — the four categories of entrepreneurial risk.

**3. Main concepts**

- Four causes of stress (Boyd & Gumpert): loneliness, immersion in business, people problems, need to achieve.
- Mind-set differences: business entrepreneurs (profit/ROI central) vs social entrepreneurs (social mission central) vs sustainability entrepreneurs (combine environmental, economic, social goals; face harder market creation due to financial-community unfamiliarity with their innovations).

**5. Processes/steps**

## Chapter 3: The Environment, the Economy and Entrepreneurship

**1. Topic:** How economic crises, climate change, and environmental/resource limits create both risk and opportunity for entrepreneurs, plus the economic theory (externalities, market failure, peak resources) and ecological frameworks that help entrepreneurs respond.

**2. Key definitions**
- **Positive externality**: uncompensated benefit to an uninvolved third party.
- **Negative externality**: uncompensated cost imposed on an uninvolved third party (climate change = the #1 negative externality of economic activity).
- **Tragedy of the commons** (Hardin): shared, unowned resources are over-exploited because individual gain is private while the resulting damage is shared collectively.
- **Emissions trading/cap and trade**: government caps total emissions, issues tradeable permits; firms trade permits based on their relative cost of reducing emissions.
- **Peak resource theory** (Hubbert): predicts when production of a finite/overharvested resource peaks then declines.
- **Mitigation** (reduce own climate impact — "inside-out") vs **Adaptation** (adjust operations to a changed climate/market — "outside-in").
- **Social discount rate**: the economic weighting applied to future costs/benefits (e.g., lives lost to future sea-level rise) relative to present costs.
- **Virtual water trade**: water embedded in traded goods (e.g., exporting beef effectively exports water).
- **Natural capitalism**: treats the economy as embedded within, and dependent on, natural ecosystem services (Hawken, Lovins, Lovins).
- **Cradle-to-cradle design**: eliminate "waste" via continuous, closed-loop material cycling (McDonough & Braungart).
- **Economic gardening**: growing local entrepreneurial capacity via public–private support rather than external investment "hunting."
- **The Natural Step**: Swedish (1989) systems-thinking sustainability framework with four non-overlapping principles.
- **Industrial metabolism** (Ayres): treating an industrial economy like a living organism converting raw materials/energy/labour into products and waste.
- **Natural advantage of nations**: Australian framework (building on Porter) showing economic growth and environmental protection can be decoupled from harm.
- **Lean manufacturing / Muda (seven wastes)**: rework, overproduction, conveyance, waiting, inventory, motion, overprocessing.
- **Ecology of commerce** (Hawken): market dynamics and innovation can *reverse* environmental degradation by pricing waste/pollution.

**3. Main concepts**
- Schumpeter's "creative destruction": recessions/crises clear out inefficient structures and spur entrepreneurial innovation.
- Five basic global problems creating both entrepreneurial risk and opportunity: **population, water, biodiversity, food, and energy** (climate change economics).
- Opportunities from demographic shifts: overpopulation (sanitation, health, food, transport solutions), youth bulge (youth-market products/services), ageing populations (age-related products/healthcare).
- Stern Review conclusion: acting on climate now costs ~1% GDP/year; inaction may cost 5–20% GDP/year "now and forever."
- The 2008–09 sub-prime mortgage crisis as an example of an ethics/market failure with global entrepreneurial and economic repercussions.

**4. Important formulas**
- Stern Review cost comparison: 1% GDP (action) vs 5–20% GDP (inaction).
- Peak timeline estimates (Table 3.4): Peak oil ~2010; Peak water ~2025; Peak coal ~2025; Peak gas ~2030; Peak uranium ~2035.
- Natural capitalism's 4 strategies; cradle-to-cradle's 3 principles; the Natural Step's 4 principles; lean manufacturing's 7 wastes (Muda).

**5. Processes/steps**
- Cap-and-trade mechanism: (1) government sets an emissions cap; (2) permits issued up to the cap; (3) penalties set for violations; (4) firms trade permits based on relative abatement cost; (5) market incentivises investment in cleaner technology.
- Historical pattern: crisis/recession → old assumptions fail → entrepreneurial innovation emerges → economic recovery (GE bulb in the Depression; McDonald's in the Eisenhower recession; Microsoft during the 1973 oil crisis; Apple/WWW during the Gulf War; iPod after the dotcom crash/9-11).

**6. Examples**
- Indonesia's palm-oil rainforest clearing — poverty relief vs. environmental disaster (world's 14th-largest carbon emitter).
- Fiji Water — exports bottled water while ~1/3 of Fijians lack clean drinking water access.
- Water footprints: 1 cup coffee = 140 L; 1 L milk = 1,000 L; 1 kg beef = 15,500 L; 1 kg chicken = 3,000 L.
- 18 of the 30 Dow Jones companies launched during a recession or bear market.

**7. Important points to remember**
- Entrepreneurs are portrayed as *both* major contributors to environmental crisis *and* the group best positioned to solve it via innovation.
- Market failure (externalities, tragedy of the commons) explains *why* pure market mechanisms alone won't fix environmental problems without added structures like cap-and-trade.
- Asia accounted for ~80% of the (2007) $47 billion global emissions trading market.
- The eight "entrepreneurial ecology" frameworks are complementary lenses, not competing theories — expect exam questions asking you to compare/contrast at least two.

## Chapter 5: Innovation — The Creative Pursuit of Ideas

**1. Topic:** The sources and process of innovation and creativity, types of innovation, disruptive technology, and innovation's role in sustainability and social change.

**2. Key definitions**
- **Innovation** (Kevin Hindle): "the combination of an inventive process and an entrepreneurial process to create new economic value for defined stakeholders."
- **Creativity**: "the generation of ideas that results in the improved efficiency or effectiveness of a system."
- **Disruptive technology** (Christensen): a lower-performance/cheaper innovation that gains a low-end market foothold, then moves upmarket to displace incumbents.
- **Invention**: totally new product/service/process (revolutionary).
- **Extension**: expansion/new application of an existing product/service/process.
- **Duplication**: creative replication of an existing concept, enhanced to beat competition.
- **Synthesis**: combination of existing concepts/factors into a new formulation.
- **Lateral thinking** (de Bono): generative thinking that deliberately breaks from established patterns; complements (does not replace) **vertical thinking** (sequential, logical, selective).
- **Adaptor** (disciplined, refines existing practice, means-oriented) vs **Innovator** (unconventional, questions assumptions, ends-oriented) — Kirton's typology.
- **Social innovation** (Stanford): "a novel solution to a social problem that is more effective, more efficient, more sustainable, or more just than existing solutions and for which the value created accrues primarily to society as a whole."

**3. Main concepts**
- Eight sources of innovative ideas: trends (societal/technology/economic/government), unexpected occurrences, incongruities, process needs, industry/market changes, demographics, perceptual changes, knowledge-based concepts.
- The entrepreneur–inventor is rare: most entrepreneurs commercialise others' inventions rather than inventing themselves.
- Common misconceptions about innovation: it's planned/predictable; specs must be exhaustive up front; it relies on "blue-sky" dreaming; bigger projects innovate better; technology alone drives success.
- Nine principles of innovation: be action-oriented; keep it simple/understandable; make it customer-based; start small; aim high; try/test/revise; learn from failure; follow a milestone schedule; reward "heroic" innovative activity ("work, work, work").
- Innovation and climate change: distinguishing incremental innovation (extension/duplication/synthesis of existing tech) from breakthrough invention; Milker's guidelines for sustainable product design (offset consumption impact; sustainable manufacturing/distribution; longer product life spans; sustainable packaging; low-impact marketing).

**4. Important formulas**
- No numeric formula; the four-type innovation matrix (Table 5.9) and the four-phase creative-process model (Figure 5.1) are the chapter's structural frameworks.

**5. Processes/steps**
- Four-phase creative process (Figure 5.1): (1) **Knowledge accumulation** — reading, networking, attending meetings, travel, notebook-keeping; (2) **Incubation** — subconscious processing via routine activity, exercise, play, meditation; (3) **Idea experience/"eureka factor"** — daydreaming, leisure environments, back-burnering the problem; (4) **Evaluation and implementation** — testing ideas with others, business-planning skills, persistence through obstacles.

**6. Examples**
- Entrepreneur-inventors: Alfred Nobel (nitro-glycerine), George Eastman (Kodak), Kiichiro Toyoda (Toyota), An Wang (Wang Laboratories), Bill Gates (DOS/Windows) — vs. Nikola Tesla, who died poor despite his inventions.
- Disruptive technology examples (Table 5.3): printing press replaced manuscripts; automobiles replaced railways; digital cameras replaced film; mobile telephony replaced fixed-line; personal computers replaced minicomputers; flash drives replaced floppy disks.
- Asia–Pacific inventions: Victa lawn-mower and Hills Hoist (Australia), Cochlear bionic ear (Australia), black box flight recorder (David Warren, Australia), refrigeration (James Harrison, Australia), penicillin's industrial process (Howard Florey, Australia, building on Alexander Fleming's UK discovery), bungy jumping (A.J. Hackett, NZ).
- gDiapers (Australia) — cradle-to-cradle-inspired flushable/compostable nappy start-up.
- Social innovation examples: community-centred planning, emissions trading, fair trade, habitat conservation, international labour standards, microfinance, socially responsible investing, supported employment.

**7. Important points to remember**
- Innovation = invention + commercialisation; the "99% perspiration" (Edison) of commercialising an idea is usually the entrepreneur's real contribution, not the invention itself.
- The four innovation types (invention, extension, duplication, synthesis) run from most to least "original" — expect questions asking you to classify a given example.
- Disruptive technologies typically start by serving an overlooked, low-end market segment before displacing established incumbents.
- Alexander Fleming's accidental penicillin discovery is the textbook example of "unexpected occurrence" as an innovation source.
